Approach

Assess. Engineer. Implement. Improve.

The same four moves every time. What changes is the discipline, and the licensed signatory the work ends up needing.

Assess

Walk the facility or read the project. Establish the baseline, the legal trigger, and which licence the deliverable actually requires.

Engineer

Size the intervention against real numbers — load, cost, throughput, payback. No recommendation survives if it fails on capital cost.

Implement

Build it, or hand over drawings a contractor can actually construct. Support through installation and commissioning.

Improve

Measure what happened against what we predicted. Feed the gap back into the next cycle.

On energy audit obligations

Kenya's energy audit obligation has been in flux. Coverage turns on annual electricity consumption — the current threshold sits at 180,000 kWh — and facilities are expected to implement a minimum share of the savings identified. We confirm the applicable cycle and threshold for your specific facility before quoting, rather than repeating a figure that may be out of date.

Who signs the work

Our EPRA-licensed energy audit team signs statutory and voluntary audits. Our in-house NEMA Lead Expert signs EIA and environmental audit reports. For design and construction drawings, we engage the registered professional engineer the work requires — they review, seal, and sign the drawings, and their name and EBK registration number appear on the document. Every regulated deliverable is signed by the certified professional whose licence applies to it.

About

Built by someone who has already tried this twice.

Ugeni Engineering was founded by Mark Wambua, an engineer with a BSc in Energy and Environmental Technology from JKUAT — a degree spanning energy systems, environmental management, ESIA, energy auditing and the policy layer around all of it.

The honest version: the first two attempts at this firm were hardware companies. One built myoelectric prosthetics and ran out of capital before it had an engineering bench. The second built tank-level pump automation, got prototypes and MVPs into the field, and stalled for want of investment. Both stopped rather than being run badly.

What those years bought is judgement: which problems are worth solving, which solutions survive contact with a factory floor, and where clients quietly get short-changed. What they did not buy was a balance sheet — which is exactly why the answer to "how do I get engineering done in Kenya" turned out to be packaging the expertise as a service rather than as a product. That is what this firm is.
